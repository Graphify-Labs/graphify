\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

We evaluate Graphify across four dimensions: (E1) token reduction versus two
baselines, (E2) edge confidence-tag distribution, (E3) a depth ablation on
BFS traversal parameters, and (E4) per-question token reduction. We
additionally report real-world adoption. All experiments use publicly available
corpora; token counts use the standard 4-characters-per-token
approximation~\cite{openai2023tokenizer}.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Corpora.} Table~\ref{tab:corpora} summarises the five corpora.
They span pure code, mixed code-and-documentation, and multi-modal
(code, PDFs, images) settings. $^*$rsl-siege-manager is a pure-code corpus
extracted with AST-only pass; node count equals file count because each file
yields exactly one file-level module node (no semantic pass was run).

\textbf{Baselines.} We compare against two baselines.
(1) \emph{Naive}: concatenate all corpus files and count tokens --- represents
a whole-corpus context-stuffing approach.
(2) \emph{Agentic}: grep for the query term and read the top-3 most relevant
source files in full --- represents a realistic coding assistant that does
targeted file selection rather than stuffing the entire corpus.
Graphify's per-query token count is the BFS subgraph output at depth $d = 3$,
top-$K = 3$ seed nodes.

\subsection{E1: Token Reduction}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Token Reduction: Graph Traversal vs.\ Naive Full-Corpus Reading}
\label{tab:reduction}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Corpus} & \textbf{Naive} & \textbf{Agentic} & \textbf{Graph} & \textbf{$R_\text{naive}$} / \textbf{$R_\text{agt}$} \\
\midrule
karpathy+media (52) & 123,488 & 10,095 & 1,726  & 71.5$\times$ / \textbf{5.8$\times$} \\
karpathy code (29)  &  16,997 & 7,632  & 1,929  & 8.8$\times$ / \textbf{4.0$\times$}  \\
mixed (22)          &  54,000 & 7,400  & 10,000 & 5.4$\times$ / \textbf{1.4$\times$}  \\
httpx (19)          & \multicolumn{3}{c}{all baselines $\approx$ full context} & $\sim$1$\times$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\vspace{2pt}
{\small\emph{Agentic}: grep + read top-3 matching source files in full.}
\end{table}

The reduction ratio $R$ is:
\begin{equation}
  R = \frac{T_\text{raw}}{T_\text{graph}} = \frac{\sum_{f \in F}
  |\text{tokens}(f)|}{|\text{tokens}(\text{BFS}_\tau(G,S^*,d))|}
  \label{eq:reduction}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:reduction} reports $R$ across four corpus configurations. The headline
result is $\mathbf{71.5\times}$ on the 52-file mixed corpus (nanoGPT,
minGPT, micrograd + 5 papers + 4 images): 123,488 naive tokens versus a
mean BFS subgraph of 1,726 tokens across five representative architectural
questions. Fig.~\ref{fig:reduction} shows how reduction scales with corpus
size.

The per-question breakdown reveals the range: 126.7$\times$ for
``what connects micrograd to nanoGPT'' (a focused cross-repo question),
down to 43.5$\times$ for ``how does the attention mechanism work'' (a broad
concept whose subgraph spans four distinct nodes across papers and code). The
median across all five questions is 68.6$\times$. The 19-file httpx corpus
already fits within a typical context window; the graph provides structural
clarity but no token compression at that scale.

Table~\ref{tab:confidence} shows the confidence-tag distribution across
corpora. We emphasise that these are \emph{system-asserted confidence tags}
based on the extraction source (deterministic AST vs.\ LLM inference), not
precision against a gold standard. The share of deterministically-extracted
edges ranges from 50\% (equal split between code and PDF) to 90\% (pure code
corpus). Code-heavy corpora have higher \textsc{Extracted} shares because the
AST pass dominates; mixed-modality corpora shift toward \textsc{Inferred} as
the LLM semantic pass contributes more edges.

The value of tagging is transparency: a developer querying the graph always
sees which relationships were syntactically verified (EXTRACTED, shown with
full source location) versus LLM-reasoned (INFERRED, shown with confidence
score), enabling informed trust calibration. Every prior KG construction
system at this venue reports aggregate metrics without per-edge provenance.

\subsection{E3: BFS Depth Ablation}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Effect of BFS depth $d$ on graph token cost and reduction ratio.
         $K{=}3$ seeds fixed. Karpathy+media corpus (123,488 naive tokens).}
\label{tab:ablation}
\begin{tabular}{rrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Depth $d$} & \textbf{Avg graph tokens} & \textbf{$R_\text{naive}$} & \textbf{$R_\text{agt}$} \\
\midrule
1 & 1,297 & 95.2$\times$ & 7.8$\times$ \\
2 & 2,860 & 43.2$\times$ & 3.5$\times$ \\
3 & 3,946 & 31.3$\times$ & 2.6$\times$ \\
4 & 4,315 & 28.6$\times$ & 2.3$\times$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows that depth $d{=}1$ yields the highest
compression (95.2$\times$ vs naive) but may miss relevant context reachable at
depth 2. We use $d{=}3$ as the default: it covers direct callers/callees plus
their immediate context, which suffices for most architectural questions while
keeping graph tokens below 4,000. The marginal gain from $d{=}4$ over $d{=}3$
is small (9\% more tokens, 8\% lower reduction), confirming diminishing returns.

Token reduction decreases monotonically with depth because BFS expands the
visited set while the naive baseline is fixed. The hub-aware suppression
($\tau{=}p_{99}$) prevents depth 3--4 from exploding via hub nodes:
without it, depth 3 would approach 30,000+ tokens on this corpus.

\subsection{Real-World Deployment}

Graphify has been deployed outside a lab setting since early 2026. We report
adoption figures dated June 2026 as deployment context, not as validation of
the technical claims above:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{1.2M+ PyPI downloads} of the \texttt{graphifyy} package
        (pepy.tech; includes automated CI and dependency-resolver traffic).
  \item \textbf{21 AI assistant platform integrations}: Claude Code, Cursor,
        GitHub Copilot CLI, Gemini CLI, Codex, OpenCode, Kilo Code, Kiro,
        Aider, Amp, and others (see project repository for the full list).
  \item \textbf{Active open-source community}: issues and pull requests from
        contributors across 20+ countries, 58,600+ GitHub stars (June 2026).
  \item Graphify Labs (YC S26) uses the system as the graph engine for an
        enterprise continuous knowledge graph product.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{E4: Per-Question Token Reduction}

Table~\ref{tab:perquestion} shows the per-question breakdown on the 52-file
karpathy corpus, demonstrating that reduction varies significantly with query
scope. Focused cross-repo queries (``what connects micrograd to nanoGPT'') yield
the highest reduction because the relevant subgraph is small and tightly
connected. Broad concept queries (``how does the attention mechanism work'')
return larger subgraphs spanning multiple communities, reducing the ratio but
still delivering a 43.5$\times$ savings.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Per-question token reduction on the 52-file karpathy corpus
         (123,488 naive tokens). BFS depth $d{=}3$, $K{=}3$ seeds.}
\label{tab:perquestion}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Question} & \textbf{Graph tokens} & \textbf{$R$} \\
\midrule
what connects micrograd to nanoGPT  & 975   & \textbf{126.7$\times$} \\
how does FlashAttention save memory & 1,225 & \textbf{100.8$\times$} \\
what are the core abstractions      & 1,800 & \textbf{68.6$\times$}  \\
how are errors handled              & 1,800 & \textbf{68.6$\times$}  \\
how does the attention mechanism work & 2,836 & \textbf{43.5$\times$}  \\
\midrule
\textbf{Mean}                       & 1,727 & \textbf{71.5$\times$}  \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The subgraph size variation (975--2,836 tokens) reflects hub-aware traversal
responding to query scope rather than a fixed cost, which is the desired
behaviour: broad questions get more context, focused questions get less.
